01 · The read

A room built in circles.

Maroon velvet, chandelier light, Western paintings, a neon longhorn — and booths that are round. That last part is the one I can't stop thinking about.

Sanjay — I'm Nelson, a creative director and shooter in Dallas–Fort Worth. I read CultureMap's piece on Mero Mero, and the room stayed with me: circular booths upholstered in maroon velvet, chandelier-style light, moody Western paintings, a neon longhorn in a cowboy hat, padded stools along the bar, and a landscaped patio for about sixty-five.

Here's why the booths matter to somebody who points cameras for a living. A round booth is the most camera-friendly piece of furniture a restaurant can own, because every seat in it already looks at the same place — the middle of the table. The blocking is done before anyone sets up a light.

And it's a new room, one I'd love to put on film.

02 · The middle

Where the skillet lands.

Chicken, shrimp, Wagyu sirloin flap, mixed — four fajitas, and one spot on the table every one of them arrives at.

Creative direction — treatment frames, not final footage
The frame

In a round booth, the middle of the table is the one point every seat is aimed at. So that's where the camera locks: one position straight down over the booth, a second at booth height with the lens just over the velvet.

Then everything lands there — the skillet still sizzling, steam lifting into the chandelier light, hands reaching in with a warm tortilla, the salsas set down in an arc around it.

The format

Shoot that properly once and it stops being a shot and becomes a format: same booth, same light, same frame, a new plate every time.

The fajitas go first. Then the shrimp aguachile, the branzino, the lamb barbacoa and the cochinita pibil each take their turn in the middle. The menu writes the series; the booth holds it together.

Stop 02 · The middle

The skillet lands.

The core of the day. Overhead and at booth height, in real time and slowed right down for the steam. Every fajita in the same frame, then the plates beyond them. This is the format — once it exists, every new dish already has somewhere to go.

Tortillas and salsas, close.

House-made tortillas and fresh salsas: the stack, the cloth, the fold, a chip going into a bowl. Small, tactile details that cut into anything — and if the tortillas are made where a camera can see, they get their own few minutes.
